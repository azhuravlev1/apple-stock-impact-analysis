Chapter one

Forty-four years in one line.

Apple listed in December 1980. Adjusted for every stock split since, a share that closed at about fifteen cents that month closed at $172.69 in April 2024, more than a thousand times higher. The milestones below are the products everyone remembers. The question is whether the stock noticed them at the time.

Chapter two

Every launch. Every filing.

To ask whether events move prices, you first need the events. I built two event tables and lined them up against 10,925 trading days.

1

Products

A Python scraper reads Wikipedia's list of Apple products: 701 releases, each with a date and a product family.

›
2

Filings

Every Apple filing on SEC EDGAR since 1994: 2,106 documents, from quarterly reports to insider trades.

›
3

Join

Prices and events loaded into HDFS and joined in Apache Hive, matching each event to the trading days that followed it.

›
4

Measure

For each event, the short-term return and volatility after it, averaged by product family and by filing type.

This was a four-person team project for DS8003, Management of Big Data and Tools, at Toronto Metropolitan University. My part was the product-launch and SEC-filing analysis shown below. Momna Ali handled HDFS ingestion and MapReduce, Steven Luc the PySpark processing and seasonal volatility, and Khushnud Ahmed the Elasticsearch and Kibana dashboards.

Chapter three

The early years moved it most.

The product families with the strongest average reaction mostly come from a time when Apple was small and nobody knew what it would release next.

Biggest average gain after release

Biggest average drop after release

Figure 2. Average short-term return after a release, by product family, for the ten best and ten worst families. The number after each name is how many releases the average is based on. From the Hive query output in the team's presentation.

The Lisa line, Apple's 68000 family, averaged a 14.2% gain across its three releases, and printers 9.8% across nine. The iPhone, the most successful product Apple ever made, averaged a 1.4% drop across thirty releases. That is less strange than it sounds. By the time an iPhone ships, it has been rumoured, leaked and announced, and its price effect is already in the stock. In the 1980s a new Lisa or LaserWriter was real news for a much smaller company.

A word of caution: several families have only three to five releases, so one good or bad week can swing their average. Modems at -13.3% rest on four releases.

Chapter four

What the paperwork says.

Filings are less exciting than keynotes, but they tell the market about money, management and risk. Each type of filing has its own personality.

Figure 3. Each circle is a type of SEC filing: average return after filing (left to right) against average volatility (bottom to top). Circle size reflects the number of filings. From the Hive query output in the team's presentation.

Calm and positive

424B2 prospectus supplements (+1.47%) and free writing prospectuses (+1.30%) accompany Apple's debt offerings. They came with gains and some of the lowest volatility of any filing type.

Big news, both ways

8-K current reports announce material events. Their average return is positive (+1.32%), but their volatility is the highest of all (2.32%), because the news can go either way.

Changes the market dislikes

Updates to existing plans did worst: 424B3 prospectus updates (-1.79%), preliminary proxy statements (-1.08%) and SEC no-action letters (-0.89%).

Chapter five

One more thing.

This is a first pass, not a forecast. The natural next steps are to compare each reaction with the market as a whole, to separate Apple-specific news from days when everything moved, to add macroeconomic indicators, and to try predictive models such as ARIMA and LSTMs on top of the event features.
